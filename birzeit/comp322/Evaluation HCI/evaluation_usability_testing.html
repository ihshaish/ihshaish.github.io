The problem with your own design

You cannot see your own interface

Once you know where the button is, you can never again experience not knowing. This is the curse of knowledge, and it is why self review fails.

The designer

Knows the menu structure, the labels, the hidden gesture. Everything is obvious. Of course it is.

The new user

Sees a wall of choices, no memory of your intentions, and a goal that has nothing to do with your architecture.

The gap

Evaluation exists to measure the distance between those two people. You cannot guess it. You have to look.

The working assumption

You are not the user. You will never be the user again. Test with someone who is.

Why we cannot trust our own judgement

Say The curse of knowledge is a real cognitive bias. Once you know something, you cannot model what it is like not to know it. Try this: tap a famous song on the desk and ask a neighbour to guess it. You hear the whole orchestra in your head, they hear random knocks. That is exactly the gap between a designer and a first time user.
Analogy The tapper and the listener. The designer is always the tapper.
If asked Does experience help? It makes you a better designer, not a substitute for users. Senior designers test more, not less, because they know how often they are wrong.
Cite Curse of knowledge: Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989). The tapping study: Newton (1990), Stanford.

Documented case · Palm Beach County, 2000

A ballot layout that changed an election

  • Names ran down two facing columns with a single row of punch holes between them, so the second name on the left aligned with the third hole.
  • Voters reaching for the second name punched the second hole and recorded a vote they never intended.
  • Over 19,000 ballots in that county were spoiled by double punching.
Why it belongs in this lecture

The certified statewide margin was 537 votes. This was not a software bug, it was a mapping failure in a paper layout that no one tested with ordinary voters. Recall Norman: when action and result do not line up, people make errors that are not their fault.

The butterfly ballot

Say The diagram is the whole story. Two columns of candidates, one shared column of holes down the middle, staggered. The second name you read on the left does not line up with the second hole, it lines up with the third, because the top name on the right claimed the second hole. So the natural action, second name then second hole, casts the wrong vote.
Numbers Over 19,000 ballots in Palm Beach County were invalidated by double punching, and analyses estimate a few thousand votes were misdirected. The certified Florida margin was 537. Present these as documented and analysed, not folklore.
Connect This is Norman's mapping principle from lecture one, on paper, with national stakes. Good mapping makes the correct action obvious. This layout made the wrong action obvious instead.
If asked Was it sabotage? No evidence. The designer enlarged the type to help older voters read it and spread names across two columns to fit. Good intention, zero testing, historic consequence.
Cite Wand, Shotts, Sekhon, Mebane, Herron and Brady (2001), American Political Science Review, on the anomalous Buchanan vote.

Industry account · Jared Spool, 2009

The three hundred million dollar button

$300m/ year
extra revenue, reportedly, from removing one barrier that testing exposed
  • A large retailer forced shoppers to register before buying. The checkout showed a Register button and a login form.
  • In testing, first time buyers were furious. They did not want a relationship, they wanted shoes. Returning customers could not remember if they had an account.
  • The team replaced Register with a single Continue button and a quiet line: you do not need an account to buy.
  • Purchases jumped immediately. One button, found by watching people, not by arguing in a meeting.
Why it matters

Nobody on the team had predicted this. They were too close to it. A handful of real sessions found money that years of internal debate had missed.

Jared Spool's story

Say Same coin, other face. The ballot shows the cost of not testing. This shows the value of testing. A retailer made everyone register before checkout. The same root cause, decades apart

Two more failures, one shared cause

Both systems were shipped without watching real people under real conditions. Both broke down in public, at the worst possible moment.

Documented incident
Three Mile Island · 1979

A control panel showed that a relief valve had been commanded shut, not that it was physically stuck open. Operators trusted the light while coolant drained away. Meanwhile hundreds of alarms lit at once with no priority. A feedback and mapping failure in a nuclear control room.

Documented launch failure
Healthcare.gov · 2013

The United States health insurance exchange launched with almost no end to end usability or load testing. On the first day a mere handful of people managed to enrol before the site collapsed under demand. Evaluation skipped at the scale where it mattered most.

The pattern

Different decades, different domains, one lesson. A system never tested with real people under real load will still find its problems. It just finds them in front of the public instead of in a quiet room with five users.

Two more cautionary cases

Say The ballot and the button are vivid but old and small. Here are two larger systems that prove the same point in different domains, so students see this is a law of the field, not a quirk.
Three Mile Island The 1979 partial meltdown in Pennsylvania had many causes, but a central one is pure HCI. A relief valve stuck open, yet the indicator only showed that the close command had been sent, not the valve's true state. Operators believed it was shut and let coolant escape for hours. On top of that, a flood of simultaneous alarms gave no sense of what mattered. This is Norman's feedback and visibility, in a reactor.
Healthcare.gov In October 2013 the US federal insurance site launched to millions and almost immediately failed. Reporting and later government reviews found it had not been tested end to end or under realistic load. The technology was fixable, the absence of evaluation before launch was the real failure.
Connect Both are the cost of skipping the cheap step. Tie forward to the five users idea: a fraction of this testing, done early, would have surfaced the worst of it.

Before you test anything

Plan it: the DECIDE framework

Evaluation is not "grab a friend and poke the app". It is planned. Six questions, in order.

D · Determine

What are the goals of this evaluation? What do you actually need to learn?

E · Explore

What specific questions will answer those goals?

C · Choose

Which approach and methods fit? Lab, field, or inspection?

I · Identify

Practical issues: who, where, what kit, how long, how many?

D · Decide

How will you handle ethics? Consent, privacy, the right to stop.

E · Evaluate

Collect, analyse, interpret and present the data honestly.

Use it as a checklist

Most weak evaluations skip D and E at the front. They run sessions without deciding what a useful answer would even look like, then drown in notes.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

DECIDE: the planning scaffold

Say This is the framework from the textbook, and it stops the most common beginner mistake, which is testing without a question. Walk the letters quickly. Determine the goal. Explore it into concrete questions. Choose an approach. Identify the practical issues. Decide the ethics. Evaluate and present.
Worked example Goal: can a new student register for a course unaided. Question: what proportion complete it without help, and where do they stall. Approach: usability test with five students. Practical: a laptop, the live site, fifteen minutes each. Ethics: consent form, anonymise. Evaluate: completion rate plus the points where they stalled.
If asked Is the order strict? Broadly yes. The front two letters protect you from the most expensive mistake, collecting data you cannot use.

The evaluation chain

Say Beginners treat evaluation as one event, grab a user and poke the app. It is a chain, and every link feeds the next. The goal from DECIDE tells you which tasks to write. The tasks shape the session. The session produces measures. The measures plus the observations become findings. The findings drive revisions. And revisions send you back to step two with a better design.
Where it sits Across the design lifecycle, the cheap links run early, inspection and Fitts style models in the design stage with no users, then usability testing on the prototype, then analytics once the product is live. Same chain, different fidelity each time round.
Why it matters A common failure is stopping at step five, a pile of findings with no revision and no second round. The grade in Phase 3, and the value in industry, is in closing the loop.

The workhorse method

A usability test is just structured watching

Put a representative person in front of the real thing, give them a real goal, and watch without helping.

What you do
  • Recruit people who match your users
  • Give them realistic tasks, not a tour
  • Ask them to think aloud
  • Observe, time, count, stay quiet
What you do not do
  • Demo the feature you are proud of
  • Explain where the button is
  • Ask "wouldn't it be nice if..."
  • Argue with the participant
The mindset

You are not selling the design and you are not defending it. You are collecting evidence about it, and the most useful evidence is usually the part that stings.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

The basic test

Say Strip away the lab equipment and a usability test is structured watching. The structure is what makes it data rather than anecdote: matched participants, realistic tasks, the same protocol each time, and a facilitator who resists the urge to help.
Joke The hardest moment in any test is when a participant is two centimetres from the button and you have to sit on your hands and say nothing. Every facilitator dies a little inside. That silence is the data.
If asked How long per session? Usually thirty to sixty minutes. Longer and people tire and the data degrades.
Cite Rogers, Sharp and Preece (2024), usability testing. Practitioner classic: Steve Krug, Don't Make Me Think.

Mathematical model + replications

Why five users is usually enough

  • Nielsen and Landauer modelled how problems are found. Each user reveals roughly the same fraction of issues, so discoveries pile up fast then flatten.
  • With about five users you have already seen around 85 percent of the problems. The sixth mostly repeats what you saw.
  • So run small, fix, and test again. Three rounds of five beats one round of fifteen.
~85%of issues
found by the fifth participant, in the classic model
The honest caveat

The 85 per cent assumes each user finds about a third of the problems, which holds for a focused interface but not for a sprawling, varied one. To measure a number precisely you also need far more people. The argument over this single figure has run for thirty years.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

The five user finding

Say This is the single most cited and most argued number in HCI. The model says each user finds a similar proportion of problems, so the curve of total problems found rises steeply then levels off. By five users you have caught most of them, and your time is better spent fixing and retesting than adding a sixth.
The fight Spool and Schroeder later argued that for large, diverse websites five is nowhere near enough. Faulkner showed the variance: sometimes five users catch 99 percent, sometimes 55 percent. So treat five as a floor for a quick formative test, not a ceiling for everything.
Distinguish Finding problems needs few users. Measuring a number, such as average task time, needs many more for statistical confidence. Different goals, different sample sizes.
Cite Nielsen and Landauer (1993), CHI. Counterpoints: Spool and Schroeder (2001); Faulkner (2003).

The skill nobody practises

Give a goal, not a recipe

The wording of a task decides whether you learn anything. Tell people what to achieve, never how.

Leading · tells them how

"Click the Registration menu at the top, choose Add Course, then type COMP322 in the search box."

You just tested whether they can follow instructions. Useless.
Goal · gives them a reason

"It is registration week. Add COMP322 to your schedule for a section that does not clash with your morning lab."

Now you see where the real journey breaks.
Why the framing matters

A good task has a context and a clear finish line, so both you and the participant know when they are done. It should be something a real person would actually want to do.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

Tasks make or break a test

Say This is where most student tests go wrong. If you tell someone the steps, you have tested their ability to follow you, not the design. Give them a goal with a reason and a finish line, then go quiet and watch them find their own path. The path they take is the finding.
Rule A good task is realistic, has a clear end state, and never names a button. Add light context so it feels real, such as "it is registration week".
If asked How many tasks per session? Three to five for a thirty minute test. Start with an easy one to build confidence.

Hearing the reasoning, not just the clicks

Think aloud: narrate the struggle

Ask participants to say what they are thinking as they work. Clicks tell you what happened. Words tell you why.

What it sounds like

"I am looking for register... maybe under this menu... no, that is settings... wait, why is there a second login box... I will just try this one."

How to prompt
  • "Keep talking, what are you thinking now?"
  • Stay neutral, never "is it confusing?"
  • When they go silent, gently nudge
It is genuinely unnatural

Nobody narrates their own thoughts in daily life, so people forget. Your job is to keep the stream running without putting words in their mouth.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

The think aloud protocol

Say Think aloud is the core technique. You ask the person to keep a running commentary while they work. The gold is in the hesitations and the wrong turns, the moment they expect one thing and the screen gives another. That gap is the usability problem, stated in their own words.
History Introduced for interface work by Clayton Lewis at IBM in 1982, built on the wider method formalised by Ericsson and Simon in 1984. It comes originally from cognitive psychology, where verbal reports were studied as data.
Caution Two honest limits. People sometimes rationalise after the fact, and talking can slightly change how they behave. Still, the insight far outweighs the noise.
If asked What if they go quiet? A neutral nudge, "what are you thinking?". Never "are you confused?", that plants the answer.
Cite Lewis (1982), IBM; Ericsson and Simon (1984), Protocol Analysis.

The hardest job in the room

The facilitator's only real skill: silence

Every word you add contaminates the data. The instinct to rescue a struggling user is the instinct you must defeat.

Do not lead

"What would you do here?" beats "would you click that button?". The second hands them the answer.

Do not rescue

If they are stuck, that is a finding. Wait. Let the silence stretch. The struggle is the result you came for.

Do not react

No wincing when they miss it, no smiling when they get it. Your face is feedback too.

The bias to name

People want to please you. They will praise a design to your face and abandon it the moment you leave. Neutral questions and a neutral face are how you get the truth instead of the compliment.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

Moderating without contaminating

Say The facilitator is the instrument, and a leaky instrument gives false readings. Three discipline rules. Do not lead with yes or no questions. Do not rescue, because the struggle is the data. Do not react, because your face and tone are feedback that changes behaviour.
Name the bias Social desirability and acquiescence bias. Participants tend to agree and to praise, especially to someone who clearly built the thing. Stay neutral so you measure the design, not their politeness.
Joke Remember Clippy, the paperclip that popped up to "help" the moment you started typing. A facilitator who jumps in to help is just Clippy with a clipboard. Be less Clippy.
If asked What if they truly cannot proceed and are distressed? After a real pause, offer a minimal hint, and log that you did. A rescued task is a partial result, not a clean one.
Cite Social desirability bias: standard in survey and HCI methodology, see Rogers, Sharp and Preece (2024).

Numbers and stories together

Measure what happened, and why

Quantitative data tells you that something is wrong. Qualitative data tells you why. You need both, or you are guessing.

Quantitative · the what
  • Task completion rate, the headline number
  • Time on task, how long the journey took
  • Errors, wrong turns and recoveries
  • SUS, a single satisfaction score
Qualitative · the why
  • Think aloud quotes at the moment of failure
  • Where they hesitated, sighed, backtracked
  • The mental model they expected
  • What they said they would do next time
The trap to avoid

A completion rate with no quotes is a thermometer with no diagnosis. You know there is a fever. You have no idea what to treat.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

Two kinds of evidence

Say Strong evaluation always pairs the two. The four numbers, completion, time, errors, and SUS, are your quantitative spine. The think aloud quotes and observed struggles are the qualitative flesh. Numbers prove there is a problem to a sceptical stakeholder. Stories tell your team how to fix it.
Callback In the replay you just watched, the numbers said completed in sixteen seconds with two wrong turns. The story said the menu is impossible to find. The story is what you act on.
If asked Which matters more? For deciding what to change, qualitative. For proving the change worked across many users, quantitative. Use them at different moments.

One number for satisfaction

The System Usability Scale, and its scoring trick

Ten statements, each rated 1 to 5. The wording alternates on purpose, odd items positive and even items negative, so agreeing means something different each time. Before you can add the answers, every one has to land on the same 0 to 4 scale where higher always means better.

Odd items · positive wording

"I would like to use this often." Agreeing is good, so the top answer 5 must become the best score. Subtract 1, and raw 1 to 5 becomes 0 to 4.

score = raw − 1
Even items · negative wording

"I found it unnecessarily complex." Agreeing is bad, so the top answer 5 must become the worst score. Subtract from 5, and the scale flips, 1 to 5 becomes 4 to 0.

score = 5 − raw

Why the two rules exist

Say SUS asks ten questions but it deliberately flips the wording every other line. Odd numbered questions are praise, even numbered ones are complaints. That alternation forces people to read each statement instead of ticking the same column straight down the page, which would otherwise wreck the data.
The whole point Every answer must end up on one common 0 to 4 scale where 4 is always good, so the ten can be summed. The two formulas are just the arithmetic that gets each question onto that scale.
Walk the table For a positive question, agreeing with a 5 is good, and raw minus one turns 5 into 4. For a negative question, agreeing with a 5 is bad, and five minus raw turns that 5 into 0, while a 1, strongly disagreeing with a complaint, becomes a 4. Point at the two columns running in opposite directions.
Mnemonic Odd, minus one. Even, from five. Then the next slide does it for real.

Field A/B results

A/B testing, and its famous limit

Show version A to half your users and version B to the other half, then measure which performs better. At the scale of millions you can detect tiny effects, but you only learn which option wins, never why.

Done well · Obama campaign, 2008

The team tested splash page variants. A "Learn more" button paired with a family photo beat the original by about 40 per cent more sign ups, an estimated 2.8 million extra subscribers and tens of millions in donations.

Taken too far · Google, 2009

A debate over a link colour was settled by testing 41 shades of blue. A senior designer resigned, arguing that data had replaced design judgement entirely.

The limit of pure numbers

A/B testing picks the better of two options you already have. It cannot tell you that a third, better idea exists, and it cannot explain behaviour. It climbs the hill you are standing on, never asking whether you are on the right hill.

COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

Analytics, A/B, and its limit

Say This is evaluation with no lab and no think aloud, just enormous numbers. You split live traffic, show two versions, and measure which performs better. It is genuinely powerful, and two real cases show both its strength and its edge.
Done well In 2008 the Obama campaign A/B tested its sign up page. The winning combination, a "Learn more" button with a family photograph, lifted sign ups by roughly 40 per cent. Scaled across the traffic that meant about 2.8 million more email subscribers and an estimated 60 million dollars in additional donations. Evidence beat the team's gut, and the gut had been wrong.
Taken too far At Google a dispute over a link colour was resolved by testing 41 shades of blue to see which earned a few more clicks. The design leader Douglas Bowman left in 2009 and wrote that a company unable to choose between two blues without data had abandoned judgement. Both cases are true, which is exactly why they teach well together.
Key idea Local versus global maximum. A/B climbs the nearest hill. It will never tell you to tear down the page and build something different. For that you need the qualitative methods from earlier in the lecture.
If asked Is large scale experimentation ethical? Not inherently, but it can cross a line when it manipulates wellbeing without consent, as in the 2014 Facebook emotion study. Consent and care scale with the experiment.

Established law · Paul Fitts, 1954

Fitts's law, made interactive

Drag the sliders and the predicted time and the cursor obey the law. Pointing time depends on just how far the target is and how wide it is.

MT = a + b · log2( 2D / W )
Difficulty 2.00 bitsPredicted 350 ms
Double the distance DDifficulty rises by one bit, so the move takes clearly longer.
Halve the width WAlso exactly one bit harder, the same slowing as doubling the distance.
Double both at onceThe two effects cancel, so difficulty and time stay the same.
COMP322 · Evaluation and usability testing

A genuine law of interaction

Use it live Drag the width slider down to its smallest and the distance up to its largest, press move, and the cursor crawls. Then make the target wide and near and it snaps across instantly. The class is watching the equation behave, not just reading it.
Who Paul Fitts, 1912 to 1965, an American psychologist who worked on aircraft cockpits during and after the Second World War. He studied why pilots pulled the wrong lever, work that helped found human factors. In 1954 he published the law that carries his name.
The finding Fitts borrowed the idea of information from Claude Shannon's communication theory and showed that aiming at a target carries a measurable difficulty: the log of twice the distance over the width. Move twice as far, or halve the target, and the task gets one bit harder.
Read the readout Difficulty is the index of difficulty in bits, the bracket in the formula. Predicted time uses simple constants for a and b so the number moves sensibly. The exact milliseconds are illustrative, the relationship is the lesson.
Why it matters here This is the predictive corner of our three families of methods. You can rank two layouts for speed before a single user arrives, which is evaluation with zero participants.
If asked Fingers too? Yes, the same law governs touch, which is why a tap target smaller than a fingertip, about nine millimetres, is a known sin on phones.

Fitts's law, applied

Edges and corners are infinite targets

  • You cannot overshoot a screen edge. Slam the cursor up and it stops at the wall, so the target is effectively infinitely tall.
  • That is why the Mac menu bar sits glued to the top edge. You flick upward without aiming and you are there.
  • A corner is infinite in two directions, the easiest place on the whole screen to hit. Hot corners and the Start button live there for a reason.
  • A right click menu appears at the cursor, distance near zero, the fastest target of all.
Feel it yourself

Click the two targets in turn. Watch which one your hand finds faster.

Design payoff

Put your most important, most frequent controls at edges and corners, or right under the cursor. The law was published in 1954 and your operating system is quietly obeying it right now.

The most satisfying idea in the lecture

Say Here is the payoff. Because Fitts's law uses target width, a target at the screen edge behaves as if it were infinitely wide in that direction, you cannot fly past it. Non negotiable

You are studying people, so behave like it

Every method in this lecture involves a real human giving you their time and sometimes their frustration. Respect comes first, data second.

Before
  • Informed consent, signed, before you start
  • Explain the purpose and that they can stop anytime
  • Ask permission before recording
During and after
  • Test the system, never the person
  • Anonymise: P1, P2, no names or ID numbers
  • Store data safely, delete it when done
The phrase that protects everyone

Open every session with it: "We are testing the design, not you. If something is confusing, that is the design's fault, not yours." It relaxes the participant and gives you honest behaviour.

No method is enough alone

Triangulate, because each method is half blind

Inspection

Heuristic evaluation, fast and cheap, no users needed.

Misses the surprises only real people produce.
Testing

Real users, real tasks, the unexpected made visible.

Slow, small samples, says little about scale.
Analytics and models

Millions of data points, or a formula with none.

Tells you what, almost never why.
The professional move

Inspect early to catch the obvious for free. Test with five users to find the real surprises. Use analytics to confirm at scale. Each method covers another's blind spot. That is triangulation, and it is what separates a real evaluation from a vibe check.

Bringing the methods together

Say Here is the synthesis of the whole lecture. There is no single best method, because each one is half blind. Inspection is cheap but cannot see what surprises real users. Testing finds the surprises but on tiny samples. Analytics sees everything at scale but cannot tell you why. So you combine them, and where they agree you can trust the finding.
Sequence The typical professional order: heuristic inspection first to clear the obvious cheaply, then usability testing to find the deep problems, then analytics or A/B to confirm the fix works at scale.
Term Triangulation, borrowed from navigation and social science, using multiple bearings to fix one true position.

The lower bar you should clear today

Watching one user beats watching none

1user
is infinitely more than zero. The first person you watch teaches you the most.
  • You do not need a lab, a one way mirror, or special software. A laptop, a real person, and a quiet corner is a usability test.
  • Steve Krug calls it "rocket surgery made easy". The barrier is mostly in your head.
  • Test a little, early, often. A rough prototype tested this week beats a perfect one tested never.
The habit to build

The teams that improve fastest are not the ones with the best first design. They are the ones that put their work in front of a real person soonest and were willing to be wrong.
