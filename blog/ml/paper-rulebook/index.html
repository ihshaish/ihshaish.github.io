The paper rulebook: writing and review rules that earned their place

Hisham Ihshaish · Machine Learning · 4 September 2026 · 7 min read

None of what follows was decreed in advance. Over the past two months Peter and I took our matched-record paper, the successor to our GE Aerospace study, through seven full review rounds before submission, and every rule in this post was earned somewhere in them: a reviewer caught an overclaim, a checking pass found a wrong number, a build shipped a stale figure, and the next round inherited the rule that would have prevented it. Readers of my continual learning post will recognise the shape of this. A ruleset maintained this way is a learning system: it trains on a stream of defects it cannot revisit, it must absorb each new lesson without losing the old ones, and a rule that stops earning its place is retired the same way it arrived. This is the consolidated rulebook our papers now start from, and the two lessons that are easier shown than told are animated below. The analysis code and archived results behind the paper that taught us all of this are public.

1. Voice: write like a person with good grammar

The target register came out of comparing my own published papers against machine-edited drafts of the same material. The differences were measurable. Our drafts now aim for a mean sentence of 26–29 words with a median in the low twenties, fewer than one sentence in five over forty words, and at most three semicolons per thousand words. That last number is the single most reliable tell: machine-edited prose joins clauses with semicolons at four times the human rate. Connective adverbs stay in, about one per thousand words, because stripping them all is itself a tell.

Beyond the numbers, the rule is to pick the adequate word, not the optimal one. A model has, reaches, drops, gives and shows; it does not attain, exhibit, render or comprise. Honest repetition beats elegant variation: say “the model” three times. Admit practicality (“we did not pursue that comparison”) instead of inventing a principle for every choice. And never manufacture an error to look human. Choose the human option only where both options are correct.

2. Words: banned, replaced, reserved

Some words were banned outright after they kept doing damage. The word “matters” is the canonical example: it asserts importance without saying what kind, and the fix is always the specific verb it was hiding (adds, constrains, bears on). It crept back into our draft twice after being banned, which is why the ban comes with a grep.

Others are reserved rather than banned. “Contrast” is kept for quantities the paper formally defines. Everywhere else it is a difference or a comparison, so the technical term keeps its force. Terms of art get one-use budgets: “triangulation” appears once, the historical anecdote is told once and never called back. And each paper picks one name for its unit of analysis and keeps it everywhere. We retired two synonyms mid-project, and the sweep for them is now part of every round.

3. Sentences: one inference each

The deepest structural habit of machine-edited drafts is the sentence that does six jobs: claim, control, caveat, inferential status and two cross-references, all before the full stop. Every reviewer objection gets answered, and no finding is ever allowed to land. The paragraph rule that replaced it has three beats: the finding, the best evidence for the interpretation, the main limitation. Then stop.

The unstacking sentence
The shorter record improves accuracy (a small margin), though the two records are produced in one process (post hoc; Section 4; Table 6), which, given the shared pipeline, cannot exclude leakage, as the appendix notes…
The shorter record improves held-out accuracy by a small margin.
The improvement survives removing the vocabulary the two records share.
The two records are produced in one process, so leakage cannot be excluded.
A schematic example, not a result from the paper. One sentence doing six jobs becomes three sentences doing one each: finding, evidence, limitation.

Two related bans belong here. A bolded mini-heading ending in a full stop, with the paragraph continuing after it, fakes structure the way a slide deck does, so the point goes into the first sentence instead. And invented compound modifiers get restructured into prepositional phrases: a large gradient across workflow stages, not a stage-related gradient. Established technical compounds (held-out, fine-tuned, macro-F1) are exempt.

4. Claims: say exactly what the table shows

Five redraws showing a positive mean is good robustness evidence. It is not “independence from the sample”, and writing it that way invites the reviewer who will read your own table back to you. The calibration rules are mechanical. A finite robustness check licenses “persisted across the five samples tested”, never “does not depend on”. A masking experiment licenses “not explained by X alone” unless it removed X completely, in which case the stronger claim is earned and should be kept, scoped to what actually survived. When a mean is robust and its magnitude is not, say which. And every printed number must carry its statistical grammar with it: one run population per table row, and captions, not axis labels, own the estimator semantics.

The discipline pays. In our final round, a checking pass found that one supplementary table disagreed with the archived predictions behind it. Because every number is tied to its source, the recomputation took an hour, the correction was disclosed in the caption, and the claim it supported came out slightly stronger.

5. Chronology: evidential status, not project history

A manuscript is not a lab notebook. Phrases like “added at revision” expose drafting history and read as a resubmission. What the reader needs is the evidential status of each analysis, and that takes exactly three labels. Preplanned: defined before the initial analysis. Prospective extension: added later, but specified before that analysis was run. Post hoc: motivated by observed results, and labelled so where it is reported, not only in a status table. Every pre-registration sentence must be provable against the public repository. A scoping a reviewer could falsify from your own git history is worse than no claim at all.

6. Figures: show the phenomenon

Our palette is fixed per paper (for this one: MATLAB blue, vermillion, and three greys), and the grammar is fixed across papers: no top or right spines, no hollow circles, solid markers, direct labels instead of legends, and colour encoding one meaning per figure rather than “third series”. No mean markers over deterministic evaluations. When a distribution’s shape carries the argument, show it empirically with a log-binned step histogram instead of smoothing it away with a kernel density.

The rule I use most is to show the phenomenon, not the statistic about it. If the claim is that a model ranking reverses, draw the two lines and let them cross.

The crossing is the claim
No legend, solid endpoints, direct labels, and the model ordering visibly changes with the record, which is the finding itself.

7. The review process itself

The rules above govern the text; a second set governs how we revise. Each incoming review is recorded verbatim and answered point by point, with every checkable reviewer claim verified before we accept or dispute it. Reviewers are sometimes working from stale drafts, and a disposition that argues with a fixed defect wastes everyone’s time. Two independent checking passes then audit the dispositions themselves, one for completeness and one adversarial, before anything is executed. Structural edits run before language edits, because language edits invalidate the anchors structural edits rely on. Fresh eyes audit each section against the rendered pages, never the source. And one final holistic read covers the whole package twice, once as an editor and once as a hostile methods reviewer who recomputes the printed numbers.

Three mechanical rules earned permanent places: build with absolute paths (a drifted working directory shipped a stale manuscript three times before the rule stuck), assert every scripted text replacement (a silent failure ships the old text), and recompute cross-reference numbers from the rendered document, never transcribing them from a plan.

8. Why this is continual learning

The connection to the earlier post is not a metaphor I am forcing. A ruleset maintained across review rounds faces the same two failure modes as a model trained on a stream. It can forget: our banned word crept back twice, which is why bans come with automated checks, not good intentions. And it can interfere: a rule added in one round (a defensive lead-in sentence, say) violated an older rule (no roadmap prose), and the conflict only surfaced when a later pass read the whole document again. The remedies are the ones that work for models, too: keep the old tests running (the battery), rehearse on the full document instead of the latest diff (the holistic read), and version everything so you can tell what was learned when. The rulebook is the replay buffer.

The consolidated rulebook lives with the paper’s public analysis code, and both of our current manuscripts now start from it. If you maintain something similar for your own group, I would genuinely like to compare notes.
